CLAIMSET A new taxonomy for vector exponential smoothing and its application to seasonal time series
licence CC-BY-4.0; converted from 10.1016/j.ejor.2022.04.040 version of record by Ivan Svetunkov, Huijing Chen, John E. Boylan; contributions C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C67, C6

DEFINITIONS
D1 VETS: Vector exponential smoothing: a state space model for a group of n time series with measurement matrix W, transition matrix F, persistence matrix G of smoothing parameters, state vector v_t with component lags l and error vector; pure additive, $y_t = W v_{t-l} + \epsilon_t$, $v_t = F v_{t-l} + G \epsilon_t$ with $\epsilon_t \sim N(0_n, \Sigma)$, written VETS(A,N,N), VETS(A,N,A) and so on; pure multiplicative, the same equations on the logarithms of data, states and errors, written VETS(M,N,N;LN), VETS(M,N,M;LN) and so on.
D2 PIC taxonomy: A classification of VETS models by which of their Parameters, Initial values and Components are common to all series of the group rather than individual, written VETS(...)PIC(P,I,C): P lists the common smoothing parameters (L level, T trend, S seasonal) and D for a common damping parameter, I the common initial values (L, T, S), C the common components (L, T, S); N means none is common. PIC(N,N,N) has everything individual.
D3 VETS-PIC selection procedure: The proposed procedure for choosing the PIC restrictions of a VETS model by an information criterion: first the restrictions on initial values, then on smoothing parameters, then on components; the ETS components are chosen beforehand when the series are grouped. Implemented as auto.vets() in the legion package.
D4 nine PIC variants of VETS(M,N,M;LN): VETS(M,N,M;LN) estimated by maximum likelihood with vets() from the legion package under the restrictions PIC(LS,S,S), PIC(S,S,S), PIC(N,S,S), PIC(LS,S,N), PIC(S,S,N), PIC(N,S,N), PIC(LS,N,N), PIC(S,N,N) and PIC(N,N,N).
D5 simulated VETS(M,N,M;LN) data: Groups of 2, 10, 20, 30, 40 or 50 quarterly series of 24 or 64 observations generated with sim.ves() from the legion package 0.1.0 from each of the nine PIC variants of VETS(M,N,M;LN) (VETS(A,N,A) data exponentiated): initial level drawn from [4, 10], seasonal indices drawn from (-1, 1) and normalised, independent normal errors with standard deviation 0.3, smoothing parameters alpha = 0.3 and gamma = 0.1 for PIC(LS,*,*), alpha in [0, 0.3] and gamma = 0.1 for PIC(S,*,*), alpha in [0, 0.3] and gamma in [0, 1 - alpha] for PIC(N,*,*); 1000 iterations.
D6 simulation holdout: The last 8 quarters of each simulated series held out, forecast 1 to 8 steps ahead from the end of the in-sample part of 16 or 56 quarters.
D7 VETS F: VETS(M,N,M;LN) for seasonal groups and VETS(M,N,N;LN) for non-seasonal groups, with a full covariance matrix of the errors and the PIC restrictions chosen by auto.vets() from the legion package.
D8 VETS D: VETS F with a diagonal covariance matrix of the errors (loss = "diagonal").
D9 ETS LN: ETS(A,N,N) or ETS(A,N,A) fitted to the logarithms of each series by adam() from the smooth package with distribution = "dnorm", the forecasts exponentiated. [source B19]
D10 ETS: ETS(M,N,N) or ETS(M,N,M) fitted to each series by ets() from the forecast package. [source B14]
D11 ETS (ADAM): ETS(M,N,N) or ETS(M,N,M) fitted to each series by adam() from the smooth package with distribution = "dnorm". [source B19]
D12 M5 departmental data: The M5 competition series aggregated to monthly frequency and to department-store level: 70 series of 63 observations; 69 used, in 7 seasonal groups (57 series) and 4 non-seasonal groups (12 series) of the same department, classified by adam() from the smooth package; a group of one series removed; trended models excluded. [source B17]
D13 M5 long holdout: The first 51 observations of each M5 series used for estimation and the last 12 held out.
D14 M5 short holdout: The last 36 observations of each M5 series used: 24 for estimation and the last 12 held out (the same holdout as the long one).
D15 MCB test: Multiple comparisons with the best on the ranks of an error measure across series; here on rMAE, with rmcb() from the greybox package. [source B13]
D16 rMAE: Relative mean absolute error: the mean absolute forecast error over the horizon divided by that of a benchmark method, aggregated over series by the geometric mean (in the M5 analysis also by the median). [source B5]
D17 rRMSE: Relative root mean squared error: the root mean squared forecast error over the horizon divided by that of a benchmark method, aggregated over series by the geometric mean.
D18 rAME: Relative absolute mean error: the absolute value of the mean forecast error over the horizon divided by that of a benchmark method, aggregated over series by the geometric mean; a measure of bias.
D19 rPinballU: Relative pinball value of the upper bound of the 95% prediction interval: its pinball loss divided by that of a benchmark method, aggregated over series by the geometric mean.
D20 rPinballL: Relative pinball value of the lower bound of the 95% prediction interval, defined as rPinballU.
D21 coverage: The share of holdout observations inside the 95% prediction interval, averaged over series by the arithmetic mean.
D22 GMRAE: Geometric mean relative absolute error: the absolute error of a forecast at one horizon divided by that of the Naive forecast, aggregated over series by the geometric mean. [source B8]
D23 multivariate AICc: The Akaike information criterion corrected for small samples in multivariate models. [source B3]

CLAIMS
C1 [formal; principal; extension; support: formal_proof E1; uses C8, C73, C9, C74, C55, C75; limitations L1; verification unchecked]
  Every feasible model of the [[D2:PIC taxonomy]] for pure additive and pure multiplicative [[D1:VETS]] can be built by inserting the block values for its restrictions on components and parameters into the block measurement, transition and persistence matrices.
  extends B6: The vector innovations structural time series framework formulates vector exponential smoothing for pure additive models.
  extends B2: Vector exponential smoothing is extended to seasonal series, for pure additive models.
C2 [interpretive; principal; support: simulation E2 artefacts A2, argument W16; uses C56, C57, C58, C59, C60, C61; limitations L3, L4, L6, L7, L8; warrant W16]

W1 [deductive] A component common to all series starts from one value, so its initial values must be common; and if the trends of the series differ, their levels drift apart, so a common level is impossible with individual trends.
W2 [deductive] A common component is one state shared by all series, which changes the measurement, transition and persistence matrices; common initial values only replace n starting values by one, leaving the matrices unchanged.
W3 [deductive] The count adds the n(n+1)/2 variances and covariances of the errors, the smoothing and damping parameters and the initial values, each counted once if common and n times if individual, and the m - 1 free seasonal initial values; as for VAR models, estimation needs more observations per series than parameters per series.
W4 [interpretive] A seasonal rise of the same percentage, such as 41%, is a natural common pattern for products whose volumes and units differ, whereas a rise of the same number of units, such as 410, is not.
W5 [deductive] Point forecasts of pure multiplicative VETS on logarithms are geometric means (equal to medians); the arithmetic means would follow from the log normal formula and grow even for a local level model, and point forecasts of pure multiplicative ETS do not correspond to conditional expectations either.
W6 [deductive] Mixed models can produce negative point forecasts and prediction intervals, which demand data cannot take, while pure multiplicative models keep a positive sample space; the paper tests no mixed VETS model.
W7 [deductive] In a mixed model, additive and multiplicative components multiply each other, so their interactions do not fit the linear state space form on either the original or the logarithmic scale.
W8 [inductive; premises C4, C57] In the simulation, common seasonal components lose heavily when the series have individual seasonal patterns, so the authors restrict them to series with very similar seasonal patterns; no experiment varies the degree of seasonal homogeneity directly.
W9 [inductive; premises C2, C3] PIC(LS,S,N) and PIC(LS,N,N) are close to the correct variant in rMAE and have among the lowest pinball values across the simulated data-generating processes, which the authors call robust; robustness outside the simulated settings is not tested.
W10 [abductive; premises C7] The restriction chosen most often combines common smoothing parameters for level and seasonality with common seasonal initial values, so the authors attribute the accuracy gains to them; no experiment compares VETS with and without these restrictions on the M5 data.
W11 [inductive; premises C7, C28] The restriction the selection procedure chooses most often on real data is the one the simulation found robust; the selection frequency does not measure robustness as such.
W12 [inductive; premises C5, C67] VETS, which estimates from the whole group, is more accurate than the univariate ETS models on these data; the differences are not statistically significant and other data are not tested.
W13 [inductive; premises C5, C67, C6, C102] VETS D is the most accurate and has the coverage closest to nominal on long histories, and it is easier to estimate than VETS F, but on short histories its pinball values are worse than those of ETS; the recommendation extends one data set to real data in general.
W15 [deductive; premises C14] The determinant of the covariance matrix, the generalised variance, falls when the variances fall, and for given variances it also falls as the correlations between series grow, so its minimisation favours both.
W16 [inductive; premises C56, C57, C58, C59, C60, C61] The PIC(*,S,N) variants stay close to the correct variant in rMAE on data from every variant, while the more restrictive PIC(*,S,S) and the more flexible PIC(*,N,N) variants each lose clearly on some data; their pinball values are worse than the correct variant's on the most flexible data, but less so than those of PIC(*,S,S). Robustness outside the simulated settings is not tested.
W14 [interpretive; premises C7, C92] On the M5 data most groups needed no common components, so the components element could have been dropped; starting from the full framework and simplifying keeps the benefits with fewer models to evaluate.

LIMITATIONS
L1 [model_specification; affects C1; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] Mixed VETS models, with both additive and multiplicative components, are not considered.
L2 [sample_size; affects C7; reduces_precision; mitigation none] The non-seasonal groups of the M5 data are few and small, so no reliable conclusion can be drawn about the restrictions chosen for them.
L3 [sampling; affects C2, C3, C4; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] The simulation covers only VETS(M,N,M;LN) on quarterly data, with smoothing parameters drawn from restricted ranges that do not cover the whole parameter space.
L4 [model_specification; affects C2, C3, C4; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] The main simulation has no cross-correlated errors; they were tried only in a small experiment with groups of 2 and 10 series.
L5 [sampling; affects C5, C67, C6, C7; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] The empirical evidence is one data set of 69 M5 series in 11 groups, each evaluated on a single 12-month holdout.
L6 [sample_size; affects C2, C3; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] Whether the restrictive variants are effective on histories as short as about one year is not known.
L7 [sampling; affects C2, C3, C4; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] Groups of more than 50 series were not tested.
L8 [model_specification; affects C2, C3, C4, C5, C67, C6; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] Models with a trend component are not analysed, neither in the simulation nor on the M5 data.
L9 [sampling; affects C5, C67, C6, C7; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] The empirical evaluation uses monthly data only.

OPEN QUESTIONS
O1 [from C3] Whether common smoothing parameters reduce overfitting because they are averaged out across the series of the group is not established.
O2 [from C85] Whether overfitting explains the narrower prediction intervals of the PIC(*,N,N) variants on longer histories is not established.
O3 [from C36, C101] Whether the wide prediction intervals of VETS F come from difficulties in estimating its many more parameters than ETS is not established.
O4 [from C2, C3, C4] Whether the simulation findings hold for monthly seasonality is not shown: the paper reports similar findings without giving them.
O5 [from C2, C3, C4] Whether the simulation findings hold for error standard deviations below 0.3 is not shown: the paper reports similar results without giving them.
O6 [from C2, C3, C4] Whether the simulation findings hold with cross-correlated errors is not shown: the paper reports a small experiment with groups of 2 and 10 series as consistent without giving its results.
